The Feynman Technique in Practice: From Knowing It to Using It

The Feynman Technique in practice — from knowing it to using it. A master coach prompt, the English dual track, a 10-point self-score, a review schedule, interview mode, and a 30-day challenge.

The last article covered how the Feynman Technique works: four steps, four AI roles, a 30-minute workflow. Knowing how to use it is one thing. Using it well is another. This is the practice guide: nine ways to make it actually stick.

One setup: your Feynman coach

Copying prompts every time gets old. Better: paste the text below once as the AI’s system prompt or custom instructions. After that, every chat opens as your Feynman coach.

You are a Feynman learning coach. Every time I explain a concept to you, play four roles at once: the student — only ask questions, never give me the answer directly, and keep asking when I’m vague; the examiner — ask 3 questions, one on the basic idea, one on an edge case, one asking for a real example from my work, then tell me which answer was the weakest; the fault-finder — go through my explanation line by line and point out which sentences only repeat the definition, which ones go in circles, and which ones are borrowed words; the simplifier — rewrite my explanation in two versions, one for a 12-year-old, one for a fellow expert. One concept per session. At the end, organize my final plain-language explanation, the core analogy, and the blind spots I kept tripping on into one structured note. If I explain in English, also correct my English expression as we go.

Once it’s set, you have only one job left: pick a concept and start talking.

Two skills at once: the English dual track

Explain it to the AI in English, and the AI does two things at once: it questions the concept itself, and it corrects your English expression (word choice, sentence patterns, pronunciation hints).

Getting stuck comes in two kinds, and they need different fixes. If the concept isn’t clear, go back to the source. If you can’t say it in English, write it down and practice it separately. Don’t mix them up — blaming your English for a concept gap, or your concept for an English gap, trains neither.

One session, two skills: Feynman trains understanding, English trains expression. Take “present perfect vs. simple past”: when to use have done, when to use did — explain that clearly, and the grammar is truly yours, with speaking practice as a bonus.

How do you know you really learned it: the self-score card

“Feels like I got it” doesn’t count. After you explain, score yourself:

Can you…Points
Explain it from memory, no notes3
Give a fitting analogy2
State the cost or the boundary conditions2
Answer a counterexample2
Make an outsider understand it in one minute1

8 or more: you learned it. 5–7: explain it again, and fix where you lost points. Below 5: go back and relearn — the concept isn’t ready to be explained yet.

Don’t let it slip away: the review schedule

Explaining it smoothly once doesn’t count. Explain it again in a few days — where you get stuck then is the real gap. The schedule is simple: re-test on the same day, then 1, 3, 7, and 30 days later.

Each time, ask one question only: state the core of the concept in one sentence, then give one example. If you can’t, go back to step three: explain it again, and add one sentence to the card. Write the number of passes on the card. Pass it 5 times and the concept is yours.

Interview mode: one real round a week

Explain a system or a concept the way you would in an interview. For example: “What happens after ClickHouse receives a query?”

The AI probes the edges: what happens if the data grows 10x? What if a node dies? Why not use the other solution? Wherever your answer gets stuck is exactly where you’d lose points in a real interview. Go back, fill the gap, explain it again.

Once a week. The concepts you’ve explained this way are the answers you can give on the spot.

Feynman’s notebook of things he didn’t know

Feynman had a habit: he kept a notebook of things he didn’t know. When he ran into something he didn’t understand, he wrote it down and went back to work through it. He didn’t wait until he needed it — if he didn’t know it, he wrote it down, then figured it out.

You can do the same: keep a “don’t know yet” list, look at it once a week, and pick one to explain to the AI. Cross it off when it’s explained, then add new ones. The shorter the list gets, the more you actually know — not because you stopped writing things down, but because you really figured them out.

Explain it right after reading

When you finish an article or a book, close it and explain its 3 key points in your own words before moving on. If you can’t, the read was wasted — go back, read it again, and explain it.

This is the answer to the problem in Episode 1: I saved 7,902 articles, so why can’t I use a single one? Because there was input and no output. Explain it right after reading — one read, one explained.

The 30-day challenge: one concept a day

One concept a day, explained to the AI. Follow the workflow: explain it from memory first, let the AI ask 3 questions, fill the gaps, explain it again, and turn it into a permanent card. A mix of English, SRE, tech, investing, and knowledge management — one full round in 30 days.

English (5)

  1. Present perfect vs. simple past — when to use have done and when to use did.
  2. Articles a / an / the — when you must use the, and when you use nothing at all.
  3. Conditionals: if I were you — why were, not was.
  4. Passive voice — when to use it, and when active is better.
  5. Phrasal verbs look up / look into / look after — how the three differ, with one sentence each.

SRE & systems (8)

  1. Kubernetes Pod lifecycle — what a Pod goes through from creation to deletion.
  2. Load balancing — where a request goes after it reaches the load balancer, and how it picks a machine.
  3. Raft consensus — when one of three nodes dies, how the other two pick a leader.
  4. Terraform state — what the state file is for, and what happens if you lose it.
  5. How Prometheus collects metrics — pull mode vs. push mode.
  6. CDN caching — when a browser opens a website for the first time, where does the content come from.
  7. Database indexes — why they make queries faster, and what they cost.
  8. Circuit breaking and degradation — when a downstream service dies, how does the upstream protect itself.

Databases (6)

  1. PostgreSQL MVCC — when two people edit the same row at the same time, how does the database handle it.
  2. MongoDB replica set — when the primary dies, how is a new primary elected.
  3. Redis persistence: RDB vs AOF — what each one loses and what each one keeps.
  4. ClickHouse MergeTree — after data is written, how does it become sorted storage.
  5. Sharding vs. replication — what problem each one solves.
  6. What is vacuum for — in PostgreSQL, where does deleted data go.

Investing (3)

  1. Compound interest — why it’s called the eighth wonder of the world, with a 20-year example.
  2. Index funds — what they actually buy, and why they’re less hassle than single stocks.
  3. Regular investing — what money are you actually making, and why keep buying when prices fall.

Knowledge management (4)

  1. Permanent cards — how a permanent card differs from an ordinary note.
  2. MOC (map of content) — how it differs from a folder.
  3. Clearing the Inbox — the four things to do in 15 minutes a day.
  4. Spaced repetition — why review after a few days instead of re-reading the same day.

AI & tools (4)

  1. LLM context windows — what happens when the context fills up, and why longer chats drift off track.
  2. RAG — how a model finds information it has never seen to answer a question.
  3. Docker containers vs. virtual machines — where containers are light, and where VMs are heavy.
  4. Reverse proxy — why so many services on a home server need only one entry point.

Check off the ones you’ve explained; write down the ones where you got stuck and explain them again the next day. One round in 30 days — and at the end of it, you’ll hold 30 cards that are truly yours.

FAQ

Does it have to be the AI? Can I just write it down?

Writing it down works too, but speaking it works better. When you write, you can pause and think; when you speak, there’s nowhere to hide a gap. The AI’s value is the follow-up questions — without one, ask yourself “why” relentlessly, or explain it to family or colleagues.

What if the concept is too big to explain?

Make it smaller. Ask the AI to scope it first: list the 3 sub-questions you must understand, and explain one at a time. If you can’t finish it, the problem isn’t you — the concept is too big.

What if I keep wanting to peek at my notes?

Don’t, on the first pass. Write down where you get stuck, then look it up after. One glance at your notes and the test is gone.

I explained it smoothly. Am I done?

Smooth once doesn’t count. Explain it again in three days. Still smooth? Then raise the difficulty — upgrade the AI to a picky expert or a debate opponent — or pick a harder concept.

Try one today

A method is worthless until you use it. Pick one: set up your Feynman coach today, or start the 30-day challenge with concept #1. If you can explain it, it’s yours.
