Dao, Fa, Qi, Shu: The Four Layers of an IT Engineer's Craft

Dao, Fa, Qi, Shu: the four layers of an IT engineer's craft. Dao is the underlying principle, Fa the repeatable method, Qi the tool, Shu the skill. Most people spend their time on the bottom two layers, and reset to zero every time the tools change.

Have you ever felt this: tools come and go — just as you master Kubernetes, AI agents show up demanding to be learned; you memorize a pile of shortcuts, yet when production breaks at 2 a.m., your hands still freeze. The problem isn’t that you don’t work hard enough. It’s that you’re practicing in the wrong order.

There’s a classic Chinese framework for mastery, four layers: Dao, Fa, Qi, Shu. Dao is the underlying principle. Fa is the repeatable method. Qi is the tool. Shu is the hands-on skill. Applied to an IT engineer, it’s a map of how to grow. Most people spend 80% of their time on the bottom two layers and almost nothing on the top two — so every time the tools change generations, they start from zero.

1. Dao: Start with “Why”

Dao is the “why” — the truths that don’t change no matter how many generations of technology pass.

First, computing is fundamentally about trade-offs. There is no silver bullet. Fast, cheap, reliable — pick at most two. Any solution that claims all three is hiding a cost you haven’t seen yet. Once you see this, most tech debates stop being worth joining. It’s not about who’s right; it’s about different trade-offs.

Second, failure is guaranteed. The Google SRE team has a saying: hope is not a strategy. Reliability doesn’t mean “nothing breaks.” It means “when it breaks, how fast do we recover?” Accept that, and you’ll do backups, run drills, and write runbooks — instead of praying.

Third, simple beats complex. One fewer component is one fewer point of failure. If it can be automated, don’t rely on someone remembering. If it can be observed, don’t guess after the fact.

How do you test whether something is Dao? See if it survives a technology cycle. The Dao of ten years ago — simplicity, automation, fast recovery — is still Dao today. The “hot tool” of ten years ago is long forgotten.

2. Fa: Turn Dao into Repeatable Methods

Dao is right, but you can’t act on Dao directly. Fa turns it into steps you can follow and get results.

Every SRE knows these: SLIs, SLOs, error budgets. The beauty of an error budget is that it translates a principle (“reliability is a trade-off”) into a number: this is how much failure you can afford this quarter. Spend the budget, and you stop shipping features and fix stability instead. No arguing — just look at the number.

Then there’s the blameless postmortem: after an incident, fix the system, not the person. Because if you blame people, next time nobody will report the incident, and the failure will just hide deeper. That’s what Fa protects — not any individual, but the team’s ability to keep telling the truth.

Infrastructure as code, progressive rollouts, capacity planning — all Fa. Fa has three traits: it can be written down, it can be checked, and it can be handed to someone else. Whether a team has Fa is easy to test: when a newcomer joins, does someone walk them through a process, or are they left to ask around?

Dao without Fa is empty talk. You can repeat “simple and reliable” all day, but if deploys are still manual commands and incidents are still handled by shouting, the Dao never lands.

3. Qi: Tools Carry the Method

Qi is the tools: Kubernetes, Terraform, Prometheus, Grafana, Go, Python, MongoDB, ClickHouse, AWS…

Tools are the most addictive layer, because they’re the most concrete and give the fastest feedback. Learn a new tool, ship a demo in a week, instant sense of achievement. Dao and Fa, by contrast, show no visible change after six months of practice. So everyone chases tools.

But tools have one cruel property: they change generations every three to five years. What you spent two years mastering may be irrelevant in the next cycle. The tools aren’t the problem — practicing only tools is.

Choose tools by one standard: does this tool help me carry out a specific Fa? If you believe in “infrastructure as code,” Terraform is a great tool. If you learned Terraform only because it’s popular, and can’t say what problem it solves for you, you’re collecting stamps.

I run 20 services on my home server — plenty of tools, but each one serves a Fa: self-hosted, reproducible, fixable by me when it breaks. Many tools, but not random — the Fa gives the Qi a backbone.

4. Shu: Skill in Your Hands

Shu is hands-on skill — the kind you can only build by doing, never by reading.

Debugging is Shu. Read the error message line by line instead of glancing and Googling. Narrow the problem down by bisection, half at a time. Before reproducing, ask: what’s the minimal reproduction? There are no shortcuts. Only after enough bugs does intuition arrive.

On-call is Shu. Woken up at 3 a.m., stop the bleeding first, find the root cause later. Stop the bleeding means restoring service for users; the root cause can wait until morning. Get the order wrong and you’re trading users’ downtime for your own sense of thoroughness.

Reading logs, capturing packets, sensing what’s wrong from a dashboard, writing runbooks that actually help — all Shu. Its trait: it only counts repetitions, not understanding. Knowing “stop the bleeding first” is not the same as calmly doing it at 3 a.m. Do it three times for real, and then it counts.

5. Most People Practice Upside Down

Draw the four layers as a pyramid. The right way up: Dao at the base, widest; Shu at the top, narrowest. Dao sets direction, Fa gives method, Qi carries it, Shu executes.

But most engineers’ actual time is an upside-down pyramid: 80% chasing new tools, memorizing shortcuts, collecting tricks; Fa is a few vague “best practices”; Dao is mostly gut feeling.

The cost of the upside-down pyramid is concrete: the company moves to microservices, you relearn; the company moves to cloud, you relearn; AI arrives, you relearn. Every technology cycle is a reset. Not because you’re not smart, but because your skills live in the Qi and Shu layers — shake the foundation and everything collapses.

Four questions to test yourself:

  1. The tool you learned most recently — what Fa sits behind it?
  2. What Dao sits behind that Fa? (Not being able to answer is normal. Most people can’t.)
  3. In your last incident review, did you change the system, or the person?
  4. Do you have a single written method that someone else could follow and get the same result?

If you can’t answer two of the four, your top two layers are empty. Time to fill them.

6. How to Practice: 15 Minutes a Day, Four Layers

Dao, Fa, Qi, and Shu aren’t four courses — they’re one daily practice. Fifteen minutes a day is enough.

Dao: read one chapter of a classic every month. Site Reliability Engineering, The Art of UNIX Programming. No need to finish the book. One chapter a month, then ask: which principle in this chapter am I violating in my work right now?

Fa: after finishing a task, write one checklist. Deploy process, incident handling, launch checks — write it down after doing it, follow it next time. Fa that’s written down belongs to the team; Fa that lives only in your head leaves with you when you quit.

Qi: go deep on one tool a year. Don’t chase new ones. When a new tool appears, ask which Fa it serves. If you can’t answer, skip it. One tool a year — after three years you have three tools you truly understand, not thirty you can only demo.

Shu: write a postmortem after every incident, even three lines. What happened, why, what changed. Three lines is enough. It’s not for others — it’s for yourself three months from now.

Closing: One Small Thing You Can Do Today

Write down the tool you use most. Then ask yourself three questions:

  1. Which Fa is it helping me carry out?
  2. What Dao sits behind that Fa?
  3. If this tool disappeared tomorrow, what would I have left?

The answer to the third question is your real skill. Tools change generations. Principles don’t. Fix the Dao first, then practice the Shu — get the order right, and the road only gets easier.
