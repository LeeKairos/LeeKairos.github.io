The Feynman Technique: If You Can't Explain It, You Haven't Learned It

The Feynman Technique — if you can't explain it, you haven't learned it. AI plays student, examiner, fault-finder, and simplifier in one 30-minute loop.

You know the feeling. You read something three times, highlighted every key point, filled notebooks, watched dozens of video lessons at double speed, and you are sure you understand it. Then someone asks you about it — and you freeze. The words are there, somewhere, but they won’t come out in order. You talk around the idea for a minute and end up further from it than when you started.

It’s not that you’re slow. It’s the trap of pretend effort: you never actually learned it. The Feynman Technique is the fix, and its standard is simple and brutal: if you can’t explain it, you haven’t learned it.

Reading is not learning

Reading feels like learning. Every word makes sense, the logic follows, and your brain tells you “I’ve got this.” But knowledge only moves from your eyes into your head at the moment you put it into your own words and say it out loud. Explaining is the cheapest test of understanding there is — no exam, no one else needed. You open your mouth, and you know immediately whether you actually know it.

The classic four steps

The method comes from physicist Richard Feynman — Nobel Prize winner in 1965, famous for explaining the hardest physics in plain words. It has four steps.

Step 1: Pick one concept. Don’t be greedy — one at a time. “Database indexes” is a concept. “Databases” is not. The smaller the concept, the easier it is to explain all the way through.

Step 2: Explain it in plain language. Pretend you’re explaining it to a 12-year-old. No jargon. Jargon is a hiding place — the moment you use a technical term, you stop having to truly understand what it means.

Step 3: Find where you get stuck. Wherever you stumble, go in circles, or glide past something vague, that’s the gap. And the gap is usually not where you can’t speak at all — it’s where you thought you understood.

Step 4: Go back, fill the gap, explain again. Read the book, check the documentation, fix the hole, and explain it again until you can do it in one smooth pass.

The AI upgrade: four new roles

This method used to have one hard part: you needed someone to listen, and someone who asks good questions. Not anymore. An AI is that listener — one that never gets tired and isn’t afraid to ask anything.

AI as the student. You explain to it, and its job is to not understand. “You said an index makes queries faster. Why?” One question like that, and “because indexes are fast” stops being an acceptable answer. You have to give a real explanation. Speak it out loud if you can, like you’re chatting — talking exposes where you get stuck far better than typing does.

Prompt:

You are a student who knows nothing about this. I’m going to explain [concept] to you. You may only ask questions — never give me the answer directly. If anything I say is vague, keep asking until it’s clear.

AI as the examiner. It asks exam questions, probes edge cases, and demands a real example. “What happens to the index if all the data in the table changes?” “Give me one example from real work.” Vague answers don’t survive it.

Prompt:

You are the examiner. Test my understanding of [concept] with three questions: one on the basic idea, one on an edge case, and one asking for a real example from my work. Then tell me which answer was the weakest.

AI as the fault-finder. It points out exactly where you’re going in circles or borrowing someone else’s words. “That sentence just repeats the definition. It doesn’t explain the cause.” Not pleasant to hear — but it’s exactly what you need.

Prompt:

You are the fault-finder. Go through my explanation of [concept] line by line and point out: which sentences only repeat the definition, which ones go in circles, and which ones are borrowed words. Be direct — no politeness needed.

AI as the simplifier. Ask it to rewrite your version twice: once for a child, once for an expert. Then test yourself: is the child’s version something you actually understand and can stand behind? Can you deliver the expert’s version from memory, without looking? The gap between the two shows you what you really know.

Prompt:

You are the simplifier. Rewrite my explanation of [concept] in two versions: one for a 12-year-old, one for a fellow expert. I want to compare them — is the child’s version something I truly understand, and can I deliver the expert’s version from memory?

A full example: explaining database indexes

Say you want to learn database indexes. Your first attempt might sound like this: “An index is a data structure that speeds up queries.” True — and it says nothing.

The AI asks: “Why does it speed up queries?” “How does the database find data without an index?”

You get stuck, so you go back and check. Without an index, the database has to scan the whole table, row by row. With an index, it’s like the index at the back of a book: look up the page number first, then turn straight to the page. No need to read the whole book.

Now you explain it again: “Without an index, the database has to scan the entire table to find one row. With an index, it’s like a book’s index — find the page number first, then go straight there. That’s why queries get faster. But writes get slower, because every write has to update the index too.”

That’s an explanation: one analogy (the book’s index), one cost (slower writes). If you can state the cost, you actually understand it.

The 30-minute workflow

Here’s how to do it. One concept, thirty minutes.

  1. Pick a concept and write down one question: ‘What is a database index?’ If the concept is too big, ask the AI to scope it first: ‘I want to learn [concept] with the Feynman Technique. List the 3 core sub-questions I must understand to really get it — questions only, no answers.’
  2. Explain it to the AI from memory, no notes.
  3. Let it ask three follow-up questions. Write down where you get stuck.
  4. Fill the gaps from the source — the book, the official docs — not from the AI.
  5. Explain it again. Shorter this time, with one analogy and one example.
  6. Turn it into a permanent card: written in your own words, in a form you could explain to someone else. Keep it in Anki for spaced review, or in your notes where you can pull it out and explain it again. At the end of a session, ask the AI to wrap up: ‘Based on our conversation, organize my final plain-language explanation, the core analogy, and the blind spots I kept tripping on into one structured note.’
  7. Come back in three days, then a week later: explain it again from memory, to the AI. If it still comes out smoothly, the knowledge is yours for good. If it doesn’t, you found a gap that time would have hidden.

Two rules

Two rules, and they can’t be broken.

First: you talk, the AI listens. The order matters. If the AI explains first, you’ve learned nothing — understanding something when you hear it and knowing it yourself are two different things.

Second: the AI can sound confident and still be wrong. Fill gaps from the source: books, official documentation, source code. The AI is a sparring partner, not a textbook.

And three common traps:

  • Memorizing a definition without explaining it is not learning.

  • Letting the AI explain while you just nod is not learning.

  • Explaining it smoothly once and calling it done is not learning — explain it again in three days, and wherever you stumble is the real gap.

Where to use it

Learning English: explain a grammar point to the AI in English — say, the difference between the present perfect and the simple past. Once you can explain it, you actually know it, and your speaking gets practice at the same time.

Learning tech: explain a technical concept to the AI — how ClickHouse handles a query, or what a MongoDB index looks like. If you can’t explain it, the documentation went in one eye and out the other.

Interview prep: let the AI be the interviewer. You explain an SRE concept, and it pushes back like a real interviewer would. Wherever you get stuck in the mock interview is exactly what to study next.

Writing, too: if you can’t write an article, the idea probably isn’t clear yet. Explain it out loud first, and the outline of the article appears on its own.

Don’t know where to start? Try these 5:

  • Present perfect vs. simple past — when to use have done and when to use did.

  • Load balancing — where does a request go after it reaches the load balancer?

  • Compound interest — why is it called the eighth wonder of the world? Give a 20-year example.

  • The permanent card — what makes a permanent card different from an ordinary note?

  • RAG — how does a model find information it has never seen to answer a question?

Going further

Real-time voice mode. Many AI tools now offer smooth real-time voice chat. When you type, you still have time to think, delete, and polish; speaking out loud is the cruelest test — every pause and hesitation exposes how shaky the knowledge is. Put on your headphones on a walk or a commute and talk to the AI for 10 minutes. It beats an hour of reading.

Turn up the nitpick dial. Once you can explain it smoothly, upgrade the AI from a curious student to a picky expert or a debate opponent. Let it hunt for holes in your argument and throw counterexamples at you — that’s what forces deeper thinking.

Try one today

You can try it today. Pick one concept you learned recently and spend thirty minutes explaining it to an AI. No preparation needed, no perfect tool required — just open a chat window. Keep the concept small: one grammar point, one command, one function. Explain one today, another tomorrow, and in a month you’ll have a stack of cards that are genuinely yours.

If it comes out smoothly, it’s yours. If it doesn’t, you now know exactly what to fix. The test is the same every time: can you say it in your own words, to someone who knows nothing about it, without looking anything up? If you can’t explain it, you haven’t learned it — and once you can, you really have. Remember: don’t let AI think for you. Make AI force you to think.

Your turn: What are you learning right now? Try explaining it in three plain sentences in the comments, and see if everyone gets it instantly.
